# Licensing and scope guardrails

**Read this before writing or editing any public-facing copy.** These are not style
preferences. Violating them creates real legal and professional exposure.

---

## 1. Publisher instrument content stays out of public copy

David is a certified user of commercially licensed assessments. Distributor agreements
routinely restrict publishing branded material — and the restriction is stricter than
most people assume.

**Never put on this site:**

- Publisher scale or subscale names (e.g. the eleven derailer scale names, the seven
  personality scale names, the ten values scale names — none of them)
- Sample reports, report excerpts, or report screenshots
- Percentile bands, score interpretations, or "what a high X means" content
- Any product's normative or interpretive material, paraphrased or verbatim
- Publisher logos or trademarks, unless the issuing body's own brand guidelines
  explicitly permit it and David has confirmed the current wording

**Instead, public copy uses the plain-language pattern names** — the Interrogator, the
Perfectionist, the Fuse, the Vanisher, the Controller, the Headliner, the Loyalist, the
Quiet No, the Visionary. These are David's own taxonomy. He owns them. They can appear
anywhere.

The private key that maps a client's assessment profile onto those patterns is the
core asset of the practice. It lives in the paid engagement and is never published,
referenced, or hinted at on the site.

**Two benefits fall out of this constraint**, and copy can lean into both: the practice
stops functioning as an unpaid marketing channel for an assessment vendor, and the
public material stays useful to the large majority of leaders who have never taken one
of these instruments.

**Describing certifications is different from reproducing content.** Stating that David
is certified in a given instrument is normally fine; reproducing that instrument's
material is not. David should confirm the permitted wording against his current
agreement before the About page goes live.

---

## 2. This is coaching, not treatment

The method borrows a cognitive-behavioral framework. Used as a coaching structure with
non-clinical adults, on work behavior, that is well-established practice. It stops
being that the moment the material turns clinical.

**Copy must never:**

- Use the words therapy, treatment, patient, diagnosis, or disorder about the service
- Imply the work addresses mental health conditions
- Promise psychological outcomes beyond work behavior

**Copy should, at least once, state the boundary plainly** and say that clinical
material gets a referral. This is on the `/work-with-me` page under "What I won't do"
and should stay there.

---

## 3. No fabricated proof

Until David has real ones, the site has none of these:

- Testimonials or quotes from clients
- Client names, logos, or case studies
- Statistics about outcomes ("87% of leaders report…")
- Years-of-experience or clients-served numbers
- Awards, press mentions, or "as featured in"

If a section's design wants a testimonial and none exists, **leave the placeholder
visible** or cut the section. An invented quote on a page selling professional honesty
is the single worst thing that could go on this site.

---

## 4. Claims discipline

The whole positioning rests on being more precise than competitors who overpromise.
That advantage disappears the moment the copy overpromises.

**Don't write:**
- "Transform your leadership"
- "Unlock your potential"
- "Proven to increase performance by…"
- Anything asserting personality change is possible

**Do write:** specific, modest, falsifiable claims. "Two or three routines." "Ninety
days." "Rated by the people who work for you." The specificity is the credibility.

---

## 5. When in doubt

Ask David rather than guessing. He is the licensed practitioner and the one carrying
the professional risk — this is exactly the category of decision that should not be
made by inference.
